### Word Count Rule

- A generated chapter must contain **at least 4,000 words** before it is returned to the calling application.
- A chapter below 4,000 words is **not complete** and must not be returned as final prose.
- After drafting, determine the actual word count of the prose.
- If the word count is below 4,000, revise and expand the existing chapter material before returning it.
- Expansion must deepen or fully execute material already required by Canon, Chapter Plan, events, objectives, POV, continuity, dialogue, action, setting, or character interaction.
- Do not invent new Canon, new events, unauthorized knowledge, filler scenes, redundant exposition, or repetitive description merely to reach 4,000 words.
- After any expansion or revision, determine the word count again.
- Repeat the revise-and-recount cycle until the prose is at least 4,000 words.
- Do not claim compliance based on an estimated target alone; perform the pre-return check on the completed prose.
- If another authoritative project setting requires a higher minimum word count, the higher minimum governs.
- No upper word-count limit is imposed by this generic rulebook unless another authoritative project setting supplies one.

### Quantitative Pre-Return Validation

Before returning prose to the application:

1. Complete the prose draft.
2. Determine the actual word count.
3. If word count is below 4,000, mark the draft as failing internally and revise it.
4. Recount after revision.
5. Check punctuation and literary-device maxima.
6. Revise any metric that exceeds a hard maximum without violating higher-priority story controls.
7. Recheck all quantitative metrics after the final revision.
8. Return prose only after the hard word-count minimum is satisfied.

The compliance check is internal. Do not include metric tables, counts, PASS/FAIL labels, or validation commentary in the returned chapter prose unless the calling workflow explicitly requests them.

## 4. GRAMMAR

- Use grammatically complete prose unless a deliberate fragment serves dialogue, thought, rhythm, tension, or emphasis.
- Maintain consistent tense unless the Chapter Plan explicitly requires a temporal shift.
- Maintain grammatical person required by the active POV Contract.
- Preserve clear subject-reference relationships.
- Avoid accidental pronoun ambiguity.
- Avoid malformed sentence joins and comma splices.
- Avoid unnecessary passive construction when a clear active sentence is stronger.
- Do not sacrifice meaning, Canon accuracy, POV control, or continuity for stylistic smoothness.
- Dialogue may use natural contractions, fragments, interruptions, and incomplete syntax when appropriate to character speech.

---

## 5. SENTENCE ARCHITECTURE

### General
- Vary sentence length and structure.
- Avoid monotonous repeated sentence patterns.
- Avoid long runs of sentences with identical grammatical construction.
- Use short sentences deliberately rather than habitually.

### Very Short Sentences
A very short sentence is approximately **8 words or fewer**.

Rules:
- Use very short sentences for emphasis, impact, tension, reversal, clarity, or rhythm.
- Avoid clustering several very short sentences unless the dramatic effect clearly requires it.
- Do not allow very short sentences to dominate a paragraph or page.
- Prefer syntactic variety over repeated subject-verb-object statements.

### Long Sentences
- Long sentences are acceptable when their structure remains clear.
- Break sentences that become difficult to parse.
- Avoid excessive nesting of clauses.
- Do not use sentence length alone to manufacture a literary tone.

---

## 6. PARAGRAPH ARCHITECTURE

- Vary paragraph length naturally.
- Avoid mechanically uniform paragraph sizes.
- Avoid repeated single-sentence paragraphs unless used deliberately for emphasis.
- Dialogue paragraphs may remain short where normal dialogue formatting requires it.
- Use paragraph breaks to support changes in action, speaker, focus, pacing, or emphasis.
- Do not split paragraphs solely to manufacture visual drama.
- Avoid dense blocks when a natural structural break improves readability.

---

## 7. PUNCTUATION

Punctuation limits are anti-pattern controls, not quotas.

### Em Dashes
- Use sparingly.
- Preferred: **0–6 per chapter**
- Maximum: **8 per chapter**
- Do not use em dashes as the default substitute for commas, parentheses, or periods.

### Semicolons
- Use sparingly.
- Preferred: **0–10 per chapter**
- Maximum: **15 per chapter**
- Use only where two closely related independent clauses genuinely benefit from the connection.

### Colons
- Use sparingly.
- Preferred maximum: **3 per chapter**
- Use only when introducing an explanation, list, consequence, or formal expansion.

### Ellipses
- Use only when narratively justified.
- Preferred maximum: **4 per chapter**
- Appropriate uses include hesitation, trailing thought, interruption of cognition, or deliberate dissolution.
- Do not insert ellipses merely to satisfy a stylistic pattern.

### Exclamation Marks
- Use rarely in narration.
- Dialogue may use them when character emotion genuinely warrants them.
- Avoid repeated exclamation marks.

### Parentheses
- Use minimally in narrative prose unless Author Voice explicitly favors them.

---

## 8. LITERARY DEVICES

### Similes
- Use selectively.
- Preferred maximum: **12 per chapter**.
- Avoid repeating the same comparison structure.
- Favor concrete, context-appropriate imagery.
- Do not force similes into passages that are already clear.

### Metaphor
- Use with restraint.
- Avoid stacking multiple metaphors around the same object or idea.
- Maintain metaphorical consistency within a passage.

### Personification
- Use selectively.
- Do not let personification dominate descriptive prose.
- Avoid making systems, landscapes, or objects seem intentional when that would create false factual implications.

### Fragments
- Use deliberately.
- Appropriate for thought, impact, urgency, dialogue, or emphasis.
- Avoid repeated fragment-heavy prose unless Author Voice explicitly requires it.

---

## 9. REPETITION CONTROL

- Avoid repeating the same adjective, adverb, image, phrase, or sentence opening in close proximity.
- Avoid restating information the reader already understands unless repetition serves deliberate emphasis.
- Avoid redundant emotional labeling after emotion has already been shown through action or dialogue.
- Avoid repetitive character-name use where pronouns remain clear.
- Avoid excessive filtering phrases such as:
  - "he saw"
  - "she noticed"
  - "he realized"
  - "she felt"
  - "he knew"
  unless the filtering itself is narratively important.

---

## 10. DESCRIPTION AND EXPOSITION

- Ground abstract information in concrete context when appropriate.
- Prefer specific sensory or physical detail over generic description.
- Do not overload passages with simultaneous sensory categories.
- Avoid exposition that repeats Canon information without narrative purpose.
- Do not convert structured Canon into encyclopedia-style prose unless the Chapter Plan requires exposition.
- Integrate necessary background information into action, observation, dialogue, memory, or narration naturally.

---

## 11. DIALOGUE MECHANICS

- Dialogue must sound distinct from surrounding narration.
- Avoid dialogue that exists only to explain information both speakers already know.
- Use dialogue tags clearly and unobtrusively.
- Prefer "said" where no stronger action or clarification is required.
- Avoid excessive adverbial dialogue tags.
- Allow action beats to replace dialogue tags when speaker identity remains clear.
- Do not force every spoken line to carry plot information.
- Preserve natural interruptions, hesitation, silence, and incomplete thoughts when justified.

---

## 12. POV NON-INTERFERENCE RULE

This rulebook does not determine Point of View.

The active POV Contract controls:
- grammatical perspective
- authorized focal character or characters
- interior-access permissions
- narrator scope
- knowledge boundaries
- head-hopping restrictions

All grammar, punctuation, sentence, paragraph, and word-count rules in this rulebook must be satisfied **within** the active POV Contract.

If a prose-mechanics rule would cause a POV violation, the POV Contract wins.

Examples:
- Do not enter another character's private thoughts merely to add variety.
- Do not switch narrators merely to improve pacing.
- Do not change grammatical person to avoid sentence repetition.
- Do not expose hidden knowledge to satisfy exposition needs.

---

## 13. AUTHOR VOICE NON-INTERFERENCE RULE

This rulebook defines mechanical quality, not project-specific voice.

Author Voice may control:
- diction
- formality
- cadence
- descriptive density
- dialogue style
- tonal qualities
- preferred imagery
- degree of interiority
- stylistic conventions

Where Author Voice and this rulebook differ, Author Voice takes precedence unless it would violate Canon, POV, or another higher-priority control.

---

Never add prose solely to satisfy a numerical target.

Do not pad with:
- repeated description
- repeated emotional interpretation
- unnecessary weather references
- redundant dialogue
- invented backstory
- invented Canon
- repeated summaries
- unnecessary internal monologue
- repeated sensory observations
- artificial transitions
- decorative metaphors without narrative function

## 15. ANTI-TRUNCATION RULES

Never remove required material solely to satisfy a numerical maximum.

Do not omit:
- required events
- required participants
- required reveals
- required restrictions
- required transitions
- required continuity
- necessary cause-and-effect
- necessary dialogue
- necessary resolution

If prose exceeds the preferred target because required material cannot be responsibly compressed, preserve required material.
